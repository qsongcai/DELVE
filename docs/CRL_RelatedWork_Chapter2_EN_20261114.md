## 2.1 Causal representation learning and the identifiability program

Schölkopf et al.~\cite{scholkopf2021} cast representation learning as a causal problem and organize the field around a single divide: representations learned under independent and identically distributed sampling on one side, representations tied to an underlying causal model on the other. Identifiability sits at the center of that program. Without a structural anchor, a representation is determined only up to a broad class of transformations, and downstream causal claims inherit the ambiguity.

That anchor has taken several forms. Comon~\cite{comon1994} defines independent component analysis as the search for a linear transform that maximizes component independence; under non-Gaussianity this anchor identifies the sources up to permutation and scale. Hyvärinen and Morioka~\cite{hyvarinen2017} identify a nonlinear independent-component model from temporal dependence alone, without interventions. Interventions supply a second anchor. Ahuja et al.~\cite{ahuja2023} show that interventional data identify the latent variables up to componentwise nonlinearities, using a degree-counting argument. Squires et al.~\cite{squires2023} work the linear case and pin down how many interventions are required when the mixing matrix is unknown, and the score-based treatment of Varıcı et al.~\cite{varici2025} reaches identification under sparsity and faithfulness through score matching. The anchors differ, yet the premise is shared. Independence, temporal structure, and interventions all identify latent components while the environment or intervention index is treated as available. That index is the object this paper treats as missing, and once it is removed none of the guarantees transfers directly.

A parallel line on the estimation side exposes the same premise. Rosenfeld et al.~\cite{rosenfeld2021} show that even a solved invariant objective can admit near-optimal solutions that do no better than empirical risk once the environments shift, a warning that the partition is no harmless input. Creager et al.~\cite{creager2021} face the missing-label problem directly and infer a partition that is maximally informative for downstream invariant learning. Their environment inference is the closest neighbor in spirit to our blind recovery, but the partition is chosen to serve a predictive invariant objective rather than to match a covariance structure, and its correctness is scored against worst-group performance. The open question is whether the index is recoverable from a pooled covariance, with no predictive objective to define it.

The unlabeled-mixture line continues along signals we do not use. Kanamori et al.~\cite{kanamori2026} recover latent components and the mixing matrix from marginal independence, a component-level result that leaves the partition of pooled observations untouched. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2026} develop sharp partial-identification bounds for a latent prevalence from large-model panels. The term partial identification enters statistics through Manski~\cite{manski2003}, where the identified object is the set of parameter values consistent with the constraints and the bounds are often sharp. Here it denotes the set of covariance environment parameters consistent with the relaxed assumptions, a descriptive framing rather than a proved sharp set, and the accompanying resampling intervals are finite-sample diagnostics rather than confidence sets with an unconditional coverage theorem.

## 2.2 Mixture identifiability and the moment-order threshold

A separate line asks what a single pooled distribution can reveal about its components. The classical result holds that a finite mixture of normal distributions is identifiable up to permutation from the full distribution~\cite{teicher1963}. Yakowitz and Spragins~\cite{yakowitz1968} state the general mechanism: identifiability follows whenever the component families are linearly independent, which reduces the problem to the linear independence of the component measures. Both statements identify the component parameters and say nothing about the assignment of individual observations.

Once only a finite collection of moments is available, the picture changes. The pooled covariance, a single second moment, is underdetermined. At fourth order the generic map for centered Gaussian mixtures is non-injective through the parallelogram identity, and generic injectivity is reached only at sixth order, within explicit rank bounds on the number of components~\cite{blomenhofer2023}. What gets identified there is the unordered collection of component quadratic forms, modulo permutation and a third-root-of-unity symmetry, neither a partition nor a finite-sample statement. These propositions draw a boundary rather than support an algorithm. We keep them separate from our own constrained fourth-order result, where whitening and a symmetric two-component frame remove the generic fourth-order freedom. The moment-order line does not imply recording-level recovery, and we make no claim that it does.

## 2.3 Environment discovery, clustering, and the covariance geometry

The line that comes closest in spirit studies environments without treating them as fully given. Yao et al.~\cite{yao2025} formulate multi-environment learning under a known index and an explicit uniqueness condition, the assumption our impossibility witness interrogates. Murata~\cite{murata2025} extends invariant-risk objectives to a clustered setting and characterizes the environmental complexity needed for recovery, while Ng~\cite{ng2025} treats nonparametric mixtures across general environments and states the conditions under which components separate.

The recovery mechanism rests on clustering theory that predates these works. Pollard~\cite{pollard1981} proves strong consistency of the $K$-means minimizer, so the empirical solution converges almost surely to a population optimum under moment and nondegeneracy conditions, and the algorithm in everyday use is the Lloyd iteration~\cite{lloyd1982}. Consistency concerns the global empirical minimizer, not the finite-initialization Lloyd path that can stop at a local optimum, and the paper keeps the two objects apart. The signal on which clustering runs has its own geometry. Arsigny et al.~\cite{arsigny2007} build the log-Euclidean vector-space structure on positive-definite matrices, which turns the matrix logarithm into a Euclidean feature, and Pennec et al.~\cite{pennec2006} equip the same cone with an affine-invariant Riemannian metric. The log-Euclidean frame is a flat space that admits ordinary nearest-centroid clustering, whereas the Riemannian metric stays on the manifold and induces a different distance.

The standard components carry no novelty. Shrinkage covariance estimation is an established family, from the linear construction of Ledoit and Wolf~\cite{ledoit2004} to the later nonlinear spectral estimator~\cite{ledoit2012}, and the log-Euclidean feature, nearest-centroid clustering, and the resampling diagnostics are equally established tools, each cited for the exact construction used. The empirical setting sits inside a mature EEG literature. Lotte et al.~\cite{lotte2018} survey the classification algorithms used in EEG-based brain--computer interfaces, and the PhysioNet resource that supplies one source is documented by Goldberger et al.~\cite{goldberger2000}. The recovered label is a source-associated technical structure, not a state of the brain, and we do not present it as a BCI classification result. Pollard's consistency delivers convergence to a population optimum but stays silent on whether that optimum aligns with the true environment, and the log-Euclidean geometry supplies a coordinate system without supplying identification. The paper states the alignment as an explicit compatibility premise and proves a risk-gap guarantee on top of it, rather than deriving the alignment from the geometry.
