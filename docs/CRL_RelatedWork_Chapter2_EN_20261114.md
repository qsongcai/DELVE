## 2.1 Causal representation learning and the identifiability program

Schölkopf et al.~\cite{scholkopf2021} cast representation learning as a causal problem and organize the field around a single divide: representations learned under independent and identically distributed sampling on one side, representations tied to an underlying causal model on the other. Identifiability sits at the center of that program. Without a structural anchor, a representation is determined only up to a broad class of transformations, and downstream causal claims inherit the ambiguity.

That anchor has taken several forms, and tracing them shows what each buys. Statistical independence is the oldest. Comon~\cite{comon1994} defines independent component analysis as the search for a linear transform that maximizes component independence; under non-Gaussianity this anchor identifies the sources up to permutation and scale. The same idea reaches nonlinear mixing once an auxiliary structure appears. Hyvärinen and Morioka~\cite{hyvarinen2017} identify a nonlinear independent-component model from temporal dependence alone, without interventions, by turning the time structure into the distinguishing signal. Interventions supply a second anchor. Ahuja et al.~\cite{ahuja2023} show that interventional data identify the latent variables up to componentwise nonlinearities, using a degree-counting argument rather than a distributional assumption. Squires et al.~\cite{squires2023} work the linear case and pin down how many interventions are required when the mixing matrix is unknown, where a global rotation adds a degree of freedom beyond the fully observed setting. The score-based treatment of Varıcı et al.~\cite{varici2025} reaches identification under sparsity and faithfulness through score matching, without recovering the mixing itself.

The anchors differ, yet the premise is shared. Independence, temporal structure, and interventions all identify latent components while the environment or intervention index is treated as available. That index is the object this paper treats as missing, and once it is removed none of the guarantees above transfers directly. A parallel line on the estimation side exposes the same premise. Invariant objectives lean on a known partition to learn a predictor, yet Rosenfeld et al.~\cite{rosenfeld2021} show that even a solved invariant objective can admit near-optimal solutions that do no better than empirical risk once the environments shift, a warning that the partition is no harmless input. Creager et al.~\cite{creager2021} face the missing-label problem directly and infer a partition that is maximally informative for downstream invariant learning. Their environment inference is the closest neighbor in spirit to our blind recovery, but the partition is chosen to serve a predictive invariant objective rather than to match a covariance structure, and its correctness is scored against worst-group performance. The question left open at the end of this line is precise: whether the index is recoverable from a pooled covariance, with no predictive objective to define it.

## 2.2 Mixture identifiability and the moment-order threshold

A separate line of work asks what a single pooled distribution can reveal about its components. Here the answer turns on how much of the distribution is actually observed. The classical result holds that a finite mixture of normal distributions is identifiable up to permutation from the full distribution~\cite{teicher1963}. Yakowitz and Spragins~\cite{yakowitz1968} state the general mechanism: identifiability follows whenever the component families are linearly independent, which reduces the problem to the linear independence of the component measures. Both statements concern the full distribution and identify the component parameters; they say nothing about the assignment of individual observations.

Once only a finite collection of moments is available, the picture changes. The pooled covariance, a single second moment, is underdetermined. At fourth order the generic map for centered Gaussian mixtures is non-injective, a consequence of the parallelogram identity, and generic injectivity is reached only at sixth order, within explicit rank bounds on the number of components~\cite{blomenhofer2023}. What gets identified there is the unordered collection of component quadratic forms, modulo permutation and a third-root-of-unity symmetry, neither a partition nor a finite-sample statement.

These propositions draw a boundary; they do not support an algorithm. We quote the rank bounds only after checking them against the source for the relevant dimensions and keep them separate from our own constrained fourth-order result, where whitening and a symmetric two-component frame remove the generic fourth-order freedom. The moment-order line does not imply recording-level recovery, and we make no claim that it does. The contrast that motivates the paper is the distance between the two regimes. Full-distribution identifiability is a strong statement about components, while a pooled covariance with no index pins down neither a partition nor, generically, the component forms, and the paper asks exactly where that line is crossed.

## 2.3 Environment discovery, clustering, and the covariance geometry

The line that comes closest in spirit studies environments without treating them as fully given. Yao et al.~\cite{yao2025} formulate multi-environment learning under a known index and an explicit uniqueness condition, the assumption our impossibility witness interrogates. Clustered objectives approach the same problem from the estimation side. Murata~\cite{murata2025} extends invariant-risk objectives to a clustered setting and characterizes the environmental complexity needed for recovery, while Ng~\cite{ng2025} treats nonparametric mixtures across general environments and states the conditions under which components separate.

The recovery mechanism itself rests on clustering theory that predates these works. Pollard~\cite{pollard1981} proves strong consistency of the $K$-means minimizer, so the empirical solution converges almost surely to a population optimum under moment and nondegeneracy conditions, and the algorithm in everyday use is the Lloyd iteration~\cite{lloyd1982}. The distinction matters for what can be guaranteed. Consistency concerns the global empirical minimizer, not the finite-initialization Lloyd path that can stop at a local optimum, and the paper keeps the two objects apart.

The signal on which clustering runs has its own geometry. Arsigny et al.~\cite{arsigny2007} build the log-Euclidean vector-space structure on positive-definite matrices, which turns the matrix logarithm into a Euclidean feature and is the exact construction behind the log-covariance representation. Pennec et al.~\cite{pennec2006} instead equip the same cone with an affine-invariant Riemannian metric, the framework underlying the Riemannian clusterers tested as a baseline. The two are not interchangeable. The log-Euclidean frame is a flat vector space that admits ordinary nearest-centroid clustering, whereas the Riemannian metric stays on the manifold and induces a different distance.

These methods share our concern that the environment is latent, yet they differ on the unit of analysis and on the signal. They operate on predictive or distributional objectives, or on the full clustering theory, instead of recording-level covariance features, and their guarantees are conditioned on the same fixed-separation premises we adopt. Everything converges on the compatibility condition. Pollard's consistency delivers convergence to a population optimum but stays silent on whether that optimum aligns with the true environment, and the log-Euclidean geometry supplies a coordinate system without supplying identification. The paper states the alignment as an explicit compatibility premise and then proves a risk-gap guarantee on top of it, rather than deriving the alignment from the geometry.

## 2.4 Unlabeled recovery and the partial-identification vocabulary

Recent work continues the unlabeled-mixture line along signals we do not use. Kanamori et al.~\cite{kanamori2026} recover latent components and the mixing matrix from marginal independence, a component-level result that leaves the partition of pooled observations untouched; their estimator is a kernel-moment-matching criterion, with $k$-means serving only as a weak baseline. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2026} develop sharp partial-identification bounds for a latent prevalence from large-model panels. Our identifiability analysis targets a different object, the covariance-based environment partition, and the two read as complementary rather than competing.

The term partial identification carries more than one meaning, and the uses are kept apart. It enters statistics through Manski~\cite{manski2003}, who develops inference under data and assumptions that only partially identify a parameter, where the identified object is the set of parameter values consistent with the constraints and the bounds are often sharp. In the econometric usage it denotes sharp bounds on a scalar parameter from a fixed set of moment or calibration constraints. Here it denotes the set of covariance environment parameters consistent with the relaxed assumptions, a descriptive mechanism-A framing rather than a claim of priority or a proved sharp set. We make no first claim for partial identification, and the resampling intervals that accompany it are finite-sample diagnostics, not confidence sets with an unconditional coverage theorem.

## 2.5 Position of this paper

The standard components carry no novelty. Shrinkage covariance estimation is an established family, from the linear construction of Ledoit and Wolf~\cite{ledoit2004} to the later nonlinear spectral estimator~\cite{ledoit2012}, and the log-Euclidean feature, nearest-centroid clustering, and the resampling diagnostics are equally established tools. Each is cited for the exact construction used rather than repackaged as an invention.

The empirical setting sits inside a mature EEG literature. Lotte et al.~\cite{lotte2018} survey the classification algorithms used in EEG-based brain--computer interfaces, including the covariance and Riemannian methods, and the PhysioNet resource that supplies one of the three sources is documented by Goldberger et al.~\cite{goldberger2000}. These frame the data rather than the claim. The recovered label is a source-associated technical structure, not a state of the brain, and we do not present it as a BCI classification result.

What this paper adds is the explicit boundary between observation mechanisms, a recording-level estimand defined apart from its window-level counterpart, a conditional risk-gap guarantee for the global empirical solution, and a pre-specified empirical protocol over three independent EEG datasets that reports where blind recovery fails. The cited work identifies components under interventions, full distributions, or marginal independence; it infers partitions for predictive objectives or states clustering consistency without an alignment premise. None of it constructs the same pooled-marginal two-partition witness, and none addresses recording-level covariance recovery under the compatibility condition stated here. That boundary is the motivation the paper is built around.
