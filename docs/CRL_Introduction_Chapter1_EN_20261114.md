# Introduction (Chapter 1, formal English)

Working title: *Identifiability and Recovery of Covariance-Based Environment Structure under Pooled Observations*
Date: 2026-11-14. Status: formal manuscript section. Every conditional qualifier is retained.

---

In causal representation learning, questions about the environment — what it is, whether it can be recovered without labels — stand as an upstream problem, one that matters a great deal for the validity of everything built on top of it. Delineating that problem brings a concrete advantage. It tells us exactly which downstream claims can be trusted and which cannot. This paper studies whether a covariance-based environment structure stays identifiable once the environment labels disappear and the observations are pooled.

Much of the existing multi-environment theory treats the partition as known. A standard formulation assumes both that the index set belonging to each environment is known and that it is unique, so identification and downstream estimation can proceed with the environment assignment fixed. That assumption is convenient and often reasonable in designed settings. It is not innocuous when the labels are gone. A pooled marginal, written $P_X=\sum_e\pi_e G_e$, carries no marker for the component that produced a given draw, and the question of whether the partition can be reconstructed from what remains is then unavoidable.

The answer, as we develop it, is not one verdict but several, and the distinctions between them are the substance of the paper. In a concrete $d=2$ construction, two genuinely different partitions induce the same pooled distribution. They are connected by a measure-preserving rotation, they disagree on half the support, and they are not related by a label permutation. This is a single witness rather than a general theorem, yet it makes clear that identifiability cannot be assumed on its own. Tighten the setting to a constrained, whitened two-component frame, and the component covariance pair does become point-identified, with every assumption stated plainly; we treat that as a constrained special case, not a generic fourth-order result. Relax the assumptions and a single value is no longer pinned down. The target can only be described as a set of structures consistent with the pooled data, which we frame as partial identification rather than a proved sharp set, and resampling acts as a finite-sample diagnostic rather than a confidence set with a coverage theorem.

On the practical side sits DELVE, our recording-level procedure. The distinction that governs the guarantees is between the global-empirical solution, which the theorem covers, and the finite-initialization and pooled-scaler variants we actually run, which are kept deliberately separate. Automatic $K$ selection, full-pool standardization, and finite-initialization Lloyd stay outside the unconditional guarantees. The procedure estimates a recording-level estimand, and the window-level and recording-level conditional means are defined separately rather than assumed equal.

The empirical work uses three independent EEG datasets and asks where each prediction holds and where it fails. A headline result is that recovery and identification of the number of environments must be assessed apart. On the balanced three-source design, fixing $K$ at its oracle value lets covariance features recover source-associated structure, whereas the pre-specified blind selector returns a smaller $K$, systematically merging the same source pair across every seed. No pre-specified automatic rule recovers the oracle value. Remove the balance requirement and the point estimate weakens further, down to the unbalanced plug-in over all subjects. These are reported as primary findings, not hidden failures. The real-data label is a technical mixture of device, task, population, duration, and preprocessing, never a biological or causal environment, and every real-data conclusion is limited to dataset-level, source-associated structure.

Tracing the problem this way brings two questions to the surface, and the paper is built around answering them. (1) Once the labels are removed and only a pooled distribution remains, is the covariance-based environment structure identifiable at all, and where does the boundary between non-identifiability, point identification, and partial identification actually lie? (2) When the structure is identifiable in principle, can it be recovered from recording-level covariance without labels, and which design conditions decide whether blind recovery succeeds or fails?

We address both questions. The first is theoretical and receives not one verdict but a three-tier boundary, with every assumption stated at the step where it is used. The second is methodological and empirical, and it is answered through a recovery procedure whose guarantees are kept separate from the variants we actually run, followed by a pre-specified protocol over three independent EEG datasets that reports failures alongside the positive result. The standard components are not where the novelty lies. Shrinkage covariance estimation, the log-Euclidean feature, nearest-centroid clustering, and the resampling diagnostics are established tools, and each is cited for the exact construction used rather than repackaged as an invention.

Our main contributions are:

1. **A three-tier identifiability boundary for covariance-based environments.** Under pooled observations with no environment labels, we state a $d=2$ impossibility witness in which two genuinely different partitions induce the same pooled distribution, a constrained point-identification result for the component covariance pair in a whitened two-component frame, and a partial-identification framing that describes the set of structures consistent with the relaxed assumptions rather than claiming a proved sharp set. The three tiers are kept as distinct statements with distinct assumptions, and none is presented as a generic fourth-order result.

2. **A recording-level recovery procedure with a compatibility-conditioned risk-gap guarantee.** We define the recording-level estimand apart from its window-level counterpart and prove a finite-sample risk-gap guarantee for the global empirical $K$-means solution under a fixed number of environments and a fixed feature transformation, with the population alignment stated as an explicit compatibility premise rather than derived. The global, finite-Lloyd, and blind variants are separated, and automatic $K$ selection, full-pool standardization, and finite-initialization Lloyd stay outside the unconditional guarantees.

3. **A pre-specified, unit-aware empirical protocol over three independent EEG datasets.** We set the independent statistical unit at the recording level and report where blind recovery holds and where it fails: source-associated structure is recoverable at a known $K$ and a balanced design, the blind selector under-counts, the unbalanced plug-in is weak, and within-source recovery is absent. The recovered object is a technical, dataset-level structure tied to acquisition and protocol, never a causal environment, and the negative findings are reported as boundary checks rather than hidden failures.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related work and positions the paper against it. Section 3 formalizes the problem, the two observation mechanisms, and the notation. Section 4 develops the identifiability boundary and Section 5 states the recovery theory. Sections 6 and 7 report the synthetic and real-EEG experiments, respectively. Section 8 discusses the limitations and concludes.
