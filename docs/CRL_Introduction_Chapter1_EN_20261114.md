# Introduction (Chapter 1, formal English)

Working title: *Identifiability and Recovery of Covariance-Based Environment Structure under Pooled Observations*
Date: 2026-11-14. Status: formal manuscript section. Every conditional qualifier is retained.

---

In causal representation learning, questions about the environment — what it is, whether it can be recovered without labels — sit upstream of everything else. They govern how much of the work built on top of them can be trusted. Pin the problem down and a practical benefit follows at once: one can tell, for each downstream claim, whether it rests on solid ground. This paper asks whether a covariance-based environment structure stays identifiable once the labels are removed and the observations are pooled.

Most multi-environment theory takes the partition as given. The index set of every environment is assumed known, and assumed unique, so identification and estimation can proceed with the assignment held fixed. The convention is convenient and often justified in designed experiments. It becomes shaky once the labels disappear. A pooled marginal, written $P_X=\sum_e\pi_e G_e$, carries no mark of the component behind any given draw, and reconstructing the partition from what remains is then unavoidable.

The answer is not one verdict but several, and the distinctions between them are the substance of the paper. In a concrete $d=2$ construction, two genuinely different partitions induce the same pooled distribution. A measure-preserving rotation connects them; they disagree on half the support; no label permutation maps one to the other. This is a single witness, not a general theorem, but it settles that identifiability cannot be granted for free. Tighten the setting to a constrained, whitened two-component frame and the component covariance pair becomes point-identified, with every assumption stated where it is used. We treat that result for what it is, a constrained special case rather than a generic fourth-order statement. Relax the assumptions and a single value is no longer determined. The target can only be described as a set of structures consistent with the pooled data, framed here as partial identification without a claim of sharpness, and resampling serves as a finite-sample diagnostic rather than a coverage theorem.

On the practical side sits DELVE, our recording-level procedure. What governs the guarantees is the line between the global-empirical solution, which the theorem covers, and the finite-initialization and pooled-scaler variants we actually run. These are kept deliberately apart. Automatic $K$ selection, full-pool standardization, and finite-initialization Lloyd fall outside the unconditional guarantees. The procedure targets a recording-level estimand, and the window-level and recording-level conditional means are defined separately.

The empirical work runs on three independent EEG datasets and asks where each prediction holds and where it breaks down. One result stands out: recovery and identification of the number of environments have to be judged separately. On the balanced three-source design, fixing $K$ at its oracle value lets covariance features recover source-associated structure. The pre-specified blind selector, by contrast, returns a smaller $K$ and merges the same source pair on every seed. No pre-specified automatic rule reaches the oracle value. Drop the balance requirement and the point estimate weakens further, down to the unbalanced plug-in over all subjects. We report these as primary findings rather than hiding them. The real-data label mixes device, task, population, duration, and preprocessing; it is never a biological or causal environment, and every real-data conclusion is bounded to dataset-level, source-associated structure.

Tracing the problem this way brings two questions to the surface, and the paper is built around answering them. (1) Once the labels are removed and only a pooled distribution remains, is the covariance-based environment structure identifiable at all, and where does the boundary between non-identifiability, point identification, and partial identification actually lie? (2) When the structure is identifiable in principle, can it be recovered from recording-level covariance without labels, and which design conditions decide whether blind recovery succeeds or fails?

We address both questions. The first is theoretical and receives a three-tier boundary rather than a single verdict, with every assumption stated at the step where it is used. The second is methodological and empirical. It is answered by a recovery procedure whose guarantees are kept separate from the variants we actually run, followed by a pre-specified protocol over three independent EEG datasets that reports failures alongside the positive result. The standard components carry no novelty. Shrinkage covariance estimation, the log-Euclidean feature, nearest-centroid clustering, and the resampling diagnostics are established tools, and each is cited for the exact construction used rather than repackaged as an invention.

Our main contributions are:

1. **A three-tier identifiability boundary for covariance-based environments.** Under pooled observations with no environment labels, we give a $d=2$ impossibility witness in which two genuinely different partitions induce the same pooled distribution, a constrained point-identification result for the component covariance pair in a whitened two-component frame, and a partial-identification framing that describes the set of structures consistent with the relaxed assumptions without claiming a sharp set. The three tiers stay as distinct statements with distinct assumptions, and none is presented as a generic fourth-order result.

2. **A recording-level recovery procedure with a compatibility-conditioned risk-gap guarantee.** We define the recording-level estimand apart from its window-level counterpart and prove a finite-sample risk-gap guarantee for the global empirical $K$-means solution under a fixed number of environments and a fixed feature transformation. The population alignment enters as an explicit compatibility premise, stated rather than derived. The global, finite-Lloyd, and blind variants are separated, and automatic $K$ selection, full-pool standardization, and finite-initialization Lloyd stay outside the unconditional guarantees.

3. **A pre-specified, unit-aware empirical protocol over three independent EEG datasets.** The independent statistical unit is the recording. We report where blind recovery holds and where it fails: source-associated structure is recoverable at a known $K$ and a balanced design, the blind selector under-counts, the unbalanced plug-in is weak, and within-source recovery is absent. The recovered object is a technical, dataset-level structure tied to acquisition and protocol, never a causal environment, and the negative findings appear as boundary checks rather than hidden failures.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related work and positions the paper against it. Section 3 formalizes the problem, the two observation mechanisms, and the notation. Section 4 develops the identifiability boundary and Section 5 states the recovery theory. Sections 6 and 7 report the synthetic and real-EEG experiments, respectively. Section 8 discusses the limitations and concludes.
